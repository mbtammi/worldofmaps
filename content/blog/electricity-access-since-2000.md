Electrification is the least glamorous development indicator and one of the most consequential. Without a reliable grid there is no refrigeration for vaccines, no lighting to study by, and no mechanised anything.

Across 211 countries, the share of population with access to electricity rose from **75.6% in 2000 to 89.8% in 2024**.

## The countries that moved furthest

- **Afghanistan** — 4.4% → **87.8%**
- **Cambodia** — 16.6% → **99.2%**
- **Solomon Islands** — 4.7% → **81.5%**
- **Yemen** — 49.2% → **86.3%**
- **India** — 60.3% → **99.9%**

Afghanistan is the one that stops people. In 2000, roughly 1 in 23 Afghans had electricity at home. Today it is closer to 9 in 10. That happened across two decades of war and a change of government, which should complicate anyone's model of how development works.

India is the larger story by population. Going from 60.3% to 99.9% closed a gap of nearly forty percentage points across the second-largest population on earth, which makes it the biggest electrification effort in the dataset by a wide margin.

## The two that went backwards

Of 211 countries, exactly two finished lower than they started:

- **Libya** — 99.8% → **77.4%**
- **Syria** — 93.4% → **89.3%**

Both had near-universal access in 2000. Both had civil wars. Libya lost more than a fifth of its coverage from a starting point of essentially everyone connected.

This is the part worth dwelling on. Electrification is usually described as a ratchet — once built, a grid stays built. These two are the counterexample, and they show the ratchet is political rather than physical. Infrastructure degrades to the level the state can maintain.

## What access does and does not mean

**"Access" is a low bar.** The indicator counts a household as having access if it can draw electricity, not whether that supply is continuous, affordable, or sufficient. A household with four hours of power a day counts the same as one with twenty-four. India's 99.9% is real connection; it is not the same claim as reliable supply, and the gap between the two is where most of the remaining problem sits.

**It says nothing about what generates the power.** A newly connected grid may run on coal, diesel or hydro. See [why the world's "most renewable" countries are its poorest](/blog/renewable-energy-share-explained) for why the obvious follow-up metric misleads badly.

**The remaining gap is concentrated.** The roughly 10% of the world without access is not spread thinly. It is overwhelmingly rural sub-Saharan Africa, which is also where population is growing fastest — so the absolute number of unconnected people falls much more slowly than the percentage suggests.
