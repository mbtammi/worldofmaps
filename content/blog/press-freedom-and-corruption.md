The argument that a free press restrains corruption is old and intuitive: journalists who can publish without going to prison will publish what officials would rather hide, and officials who expect to be exposed steal less.

The country data supports it. Across 170 countries the two indices correlate at **−0.61**, or about **37% of shared variation**.

But this is a case where the caveats are as important as the finding, so read to the end before quoting the number.

## Read the scales carefully — they point opposite ways

This trips people up constantly, including on ranking pages that sort both indices the same direction.

**Press freedom** here is the Reporters Without Borders index, where a **lower score is better**. Norway is best in the dataset at **6.72**. Eritrea is worst at **81.45**.

**Corruption perception** is Transparency International's CPI, where a **higher score is better** — it measures perceived *cleanliness*, not perceived corruption. Denmark leads at **90**. South Sudan is last at **8**.

So the negative correlation is the expected direction: countries with low (good) press-freedom scores have high (clean) CPI scores. If you ever see one of these two ranked "highest first" and described as a leaderboard, check which way the index runs before believing it.

## The two ends

Free press, clean government:

- **Norway** — press freedom **6.72**, corruption score **81**
- **Finland** — **6.99**, **88**
- **Sweden** — **7.24**, **80**
- **Denmark** — **8.57**, **90**
- **Netherlands** — **9.67**, **78**
- **New Zealand** — **10.04**, **83**

Controlled press, perceived corruption:

- **Eritrea** — press freedom **81.45**, corruption score **13**
- **North Korea** — **81.28**, **15**
- **Turkmenistan** — **80.03**, **17**
- **Syria** — **70.63**, **12**
- **Yemen** — **62.35**, **13**
- **Somalia** — **55.47**, **9**

The Nordic sweep is close to total, and the bottom of the table is a list of closed authoritarian states and countries in prolonged conflict.

## The problem with taking −0.61 at face value

Three issues, in increasing order of seriousness.

**The snapshots are three years apart.** The press freedom figures here are from **2021**; the corruption figures are from **2024**. Press freedom in particular can shift fast — a coup, an emergency law, the jailing of an editor — so some of the pairs are comparing different periods of the same country.

**Both are composite indices, not measurements.** Neither counts anything directly. Both aggregate expert assessments and survey responses into a single number on an arbitrary scale. A country's score of 78 versus 80 is not a meaningful difference; a score of 15 versus 80 is.

**Both partly rely on the same kind of respondent.** The CPI is explicitly a *perceptions* index, built from country-risk assessments by analysts and business surveys. Press freedom scoring draws on journalists and media-freedom experts. These populations read each other's work. When a country is widely reported as repressive, that reporting plausibly moves both scores — which would produce a correlation between the indices without any underlying causal link between press freedom and actual corruption.

This is a real methodological concern, not a pedantic one. It does not mean the finding is wrong. It does mean **−0.61 is an upper bound on the true relationship**, and the honest reading is "these two indices agree with each other", which is a weaker claim than "a free press reduces corruption."

## What would strengthen the case

The evidence that free media actually reduces corruption — as opposed to co-occurring with low corruption — comes mostly from natural experiments within single countries: what happens to procurement fraud after a local newspaper closes, or after a broadcast licence is revoked. That work generally does find a causal effect, and it is considerably more convincing than any cross-country correlation, including this one.

Cross-country comparison is good at showing you where to look. It is poor at telling you what causes what, and a correlation of −0.61 between two expert-survey indices is exactly the kind of number that gets over-quoted.

## Direction of causation, if any

Even taking the relationship as real, it plausibly runs both ways. Free press constrains corruption; entrenched corruption also gives officials both motive and means to capture or suppress media. Countries rarely move along one axis without the other.
