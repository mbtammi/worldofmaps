Life expectancy moves slowly. A country adding two or three years over a decade is doing well. Which is what makes the top of this table so strange.

Across 217 countries, average life expectancy at birth rose from **67.6 years in 2000 to 74.0 in 2024**. That global figure hides enormous variation, because the gain was not spread evenly — it was concentrated almost entirely in the countries that started furthest behind.

## The largest gains

- **Malawi** — 46.2 → **67.6 years**, a gain of **21.4 years**
- **Rwanda** — 47.8 → **68.0**, a gain of 20.2
- **Zambia** — 46.6 → **66.5**, a gain of 19.9
- **Ethiopia** — 50.9 → **67.6**, a gain of 16.7

Malawi added more than two decades of life expectancy in two and a half. For comparison, it took the United Kingdom roughly a century to make the same gain historically.

Three of those four are in southern and eastern Africa, and that is the clue. These are the countries hit hardest by HIV/AIDS in the 1990s, when life expectancy in parts of the region collapsed into the forties. What this table measures is substantially the arrival of antiretroviral therapy. The gain is not simply "development" — it is one specific medical intervention reaching tens of millions of people.

Malawi at 46.2 years in 2000 was not Malawi's natural baseline. It was Malawi mid-epidemic. A large part of the 21-year gain is recovery to where the country would otherwise have been, which makes it no less real but does change what it is evidence of.

## The two that ended lower

Of 217 countries, exactly **two** finished below their 2000 figure:

- **Palestine** — 70.3 → **69.2 years**
- **Dominica** — 71.7 → **71.3**

That is the whole list. Dominica's movement is small enough to be noise on a population of about 67,000. Palestine's is not noise.

Notably, **Syria is not on this list** — 70.9 → 72.6, a net gain despite the civil war, though the trajectory within those years is far from a straight line. Annual figures matter here in a way the endpoints conceal.

## The ceiling is real

At the other end, **Monaco** went from 82.1 to **86.5 years** — the highest in the world, and a gain of only 4.4 years over the same period. Rich countries are pressing against a biological and practical ceiling where each additional year costs far more than the last.

This is the single most consistent pattern in global development data: **the gap closes from the bottom, not the top.** Nigeria is the reminder that it does not close everywhere — 47.1 → 54.6 is progress, but it leaves the country roughly 32 years behind Monaco.

## Reading this number honestly

**Life expectancy at birth is not a prediction.** It is a synthetic figure: how long a baby born this year would live *if* today's age-specific death rates held for its whole life. They will not. It is a snapshot of current mortality dressed as a forecast.

**It is dragged hard by infant deaths.** A country that halves child mortality adds years to this number without anyone living noticeably longer in old age. Much of the African gain above is exactly that, which is why it tracks so closely with [the fall in child mortality](/blog/child-mortality-since-2000).
